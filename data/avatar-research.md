YouTube creator ecosystem:

- **Vacuum Wars (@VacuumWars)** — buys at retail, no sponsored reviews, publishes full methodology (airflow anemometer, suction manometer, 2.5-inch flattened-hair pickup test, 7-inch hair-tangle test, 9-stain coffee + 5-stain grape-juice mopping). Strongest authority for the Labor Math Desk.

## Avatars

### Avatar: Practical Floor Keeper

#### Core job
"Give me one good tool for normal weekly floor care without turning this into a research project or a hobby."

#### Emotional state at arrival
Mildly impatient. Overwhelmed by the aisle/search results. Wants the winner, not the education. Has maybe 10 minutes. Slightly embarrassed at having put off replacing a broken or inherited vacuum for too long.

#### Typical traits
25-50. Mix of renter and small-to-mid homeowner. 800-2000 sqft of living space. Dual-income or single-earner apartment. Their current vacuum is broken, heavy, or hand-me-down. They want a replacement that's easier to grab for a 5-minute tidy-up. Does NOT see themselves as "a vacuum person." Checks Consumer Reports + Good Housekeeping + a Wirecutter roundup + one Reddit thread before buying. Cross-references 3-4 sources maximum; decision happens within 1-2 sessions.

#### What they search
`best cordless vacuum under 200`, `best stick vacuum 2026`, `shark vs dyson cordless`, `is dyson worth it`, `best cordless vacuum for apartment`, `cordless vacuum hardwood and carpet`, `best handheld vacuum for stairs`, `simple mop for hardwood`.

Mental model: "I want the thing 80% of normal people are happy with at the price most normal people pay."

#### Budget and decision factors
Budget band: **$80 floor, $150-250 sweet spot, $400 stretch, $600 is aspirational.** Dyson at $600+ feels like brand tax they might be paying.

Cares about: weight (can they maneuver it upstairs), real-world battery runtime (not marketing minutes), bin size (emptying mid-clean = bad), hair-wrap resistance on brushroll (even if not pet-primary, someone in the house has long hair), filter replacement cost over 3 years. Good Housekeeping + Consumer Reports as tiebreakers.

Does NOT care about: suction specs over ~20kPa, LiDAR mapping, smart-home app integration, styling.

#### Dealbreakers
- Proprietary batteries that cost $100+ in year 3 and are hard to find.
- Bins that require disassembly to empty (Dyson's click-release is baseline).
- Hair wrap that requires scissors weekly.
- Budget models with no sealed-filtration (releases dust back into the room — Vacuum Wars calls this out regularly).
- "Check price" with no actual price — they bounce immediately.

#### Recurring concerns
- "Is the lightweight version actually light enough?" (stairs + ceiling fans test)
- Can this be my ONLY vacuum, or do I need a backup? (apartment-dweller subset)
- Battery degrading in year 2 — what's the replacement cost?
- "Shark vs Dyson" is the forever debate — they want a clear tiebreaker, not both-sides-ism.

#### Voice and page-structure fit
Tone: direct, calm, anti-jargon, warm but not chummy. Lead with price + use-case ("best under $200 for hard floors + area rugs"). Simple tradeoff table: weight / runtime / bin size / brushroll type / filter cost over 3 years. Name Consumer Reports + Good Housekeeping Institute as trust anchors. Include "will this replace my current vacuum or supplement it?" decision prompt early. Top pick → runner-up → budget → splurge. Explicit "who shouldn't buy this" per pick. Avoid methodology deep-dives — those belong to the Labor Math Desk's pages.

### Avatar: Labor Trader

#### Core job
"I will spend more money to reclaim hours of my life — but prove to me this tool doesn't cost me those hours right back in maintenance."

#### Emotional state at arrival
Tired. Overwhelmed by household-labor load. Skeptical of gadget marketing because they've been burned before. Cautiously willing to spend $500-1500 if the time-math works. Scared the purchase becomes a second chore (dock upkeep, firmware fights, mop-pad smell).

#### Typical traits
30-55. Dual-income or single high-earner. Homeowner or long-term renter. 1200-3500 sqft. Trigger is usually a life-load shift: new baby, return-to-office schedule, aging knees, partner's work ramp. Tech-adjacent but not a tinkerer. Owns a primary vacuum that still works — the new buy is an addition, not a replacement. Comfortable with apps and firmware but has a real breaking point when dock maintenance becomes weekly work. Talks to peers who already own the category.

#### What they search
`best robot vacuum 2026`, `best robot vacuum for hardwood`, `roborock qrevo vs saros`, `is a robot vacuum worth it`, `robot vacuum self-empty worth it`, `robot vacuum dock mold`, `dirty water tank smell roborock`, `roborock vs eufy vs shark`.

Mental model: "I'm buying hours of my life back. Prove to me this one doesn't cost me those hours back in maintenance."

#### Budget and decision factors
Budget band: **$300 entry, $500-800 mainstream target, up to $1500 for premium docks.** Willing to stretch when the premium tier removes meaningful labor (LiDAR over gyro, self-empty over manual, auto-mop-wash over hand-rinse).

Cares about: dock reliability (not just "has a dock" — does it dry, does wastewater empty cleanly, does clean-water refill without leaking), obstacle avoidance that actually works on socks + cords, hair-tangle resistance on main brush, LiDAR + mapping, app stability across firmware updates, OTA support 18 months in. Methodology-backed reviews (Vacuum Wars' 7-inch hair-tangle test, Modern Castle's 12-debris matrix) carry more weight than CR/GHI.

Does NOT care about: bare Pa suction specs (learned these are marketing), accessory counts beyond 1-2 useful pieces, "smart home integration" beyond basic scheduling.

#### Dealbreakers
- Dock becomes a second chore. Mop pads that sit wet and grow smell. Dirty-water tanks that require scrubbing. Bags that jam on hair.
- App requires cloud account + vague data-privacy policy to schedule basic runs (rising concern post-2024 Shenzhen cycles).
- Post-warranty abandonment — "poor support + breakdowns" kills the rebuy decision for unit #2 upstairs.
- Obstacle-avoidance marketing that doesn't match reality. If it eats cords, socks, or pet accidents, the whole labor-removal thesis collapses.

#### Recurring concerns
- Long-tail dock maintenance — how often am I actually cleaning this thing?
- Carpet transition — does it climb a low-pile rug without getting stuck, does the mop lift so carpet doesn't get soaked?
- Firmware regressions — "it worked until the February update" is a recurring thread pattern.
- Resale / upgrade path — they know they'll upgrade in 3-4 years. Brand that holds value or earns trust for rebuy matters.
- "My friend said theirs was useless" — they've heard one horror story and need counter-evidence, not marketing.

#### Voice and page-structure fit
Tone: candid, tradeoff-forward, specific. Lead with the labor math ("what does it actually remove from your week") before the suction spec. Name the dock-maintenance reality up front — don't bury it. Use an explicit "what this doesn't do" block. Cite named methodology (Vacuum Wars' 7-inch hair-tangle test, Modern Castle's 12-debris matrix) as the "how do we know" evidence.

Page structure: short executive pick + 2 alternates (budget / splurge), explicit tradeoff table (dock reliability, hair-tangle, navigation type, app-privacy posture, post-warranty update history), maintenance-reality block, then detailed picks. Avoid listicle bloat — they bounce on "25 best robot vacuums." Vacuum Wars methodology is the exclusive trust source for this desk.

### Avatar: Surface Protector

#### Core job
"I've been burned once. Tell me what will NOT damage my engineered hardwood / laminate / natural stone / grout / stainless — then tell me the safest thing that actually works."

#### Emotional state at arrival
Anxious. Methodical when in research mode. Often in "I already screwed up once" mode — they tried something that seemed fine and damaged a floor, etched a counter, left a haze. Now over-researches to protect sunk-cost surfaces. Reads flooring manufacturer's care guide BEFORE buying a cleaner.

#### Typical traits
35-60. Recent homeowner (3-8 years in). Specific sunk-cost surfaces — engineered hardwood, laminate, natural stone countertops, grout in tile floors, stainless appliances. Cares about ingredient lists, pH, manufacturer warranty implications. Suspicious of "one cleaner for everything." Cross-references r/HomeImprovement, Houzz, and the flooring manufacturer's website in every decision.

#### What they search
`can you use a steam mop on engineered hardwood`, `steam mop laminate damage`, `best hardwood floor cleaner non toxic`, `bona vs [competitor] hardwood`, `best grout cleaner for tile floors`, `stainless steel cleaner streak free`, `[surface] cleaner manufacturer approved`, `what NOT to use on [surface]`.

Mental model: "Tell me what will ruin this, and then tell me the safest thing that actually works."

#### Budget and decision factors
Budget band: **$15-40 for chemistry products, $80-150 for steam mops, $50-100 for specialty tools.** Willing to pay for safety — "Bona is $20, a replacement plank is $200, this isn't a decision."

Cares about: manufacturer-of-the-surface endorsement (Bona co-marketed with hardwood manufacturers matters), pH (pH-neutral is the operative phrase), ingredient transparency (EWG rating, plant-based claims), streak-free performance, genuine anti-mold claims on grout, warranty implications. Exclusive trust lane: flooring-manufacturer care guides + EWG Verified + Made Safe + chemistry YouTubers (Daniel Brown, Mr. Housekeeping).

Does NOT care about: aggressive cleaning-power claims (suspicious), scent marketing (often wants unscented), "professional-grade" framing, brand prestige.

#### Dealbreakers
- Any page claiming a steam mop is safe on engineered hardwood or laminate without MASSIVE caveats. Houzz-documented case: "planks beginning to bubble at the seams within months of weekly steam mop use." Most laminate manufacturers void the warranty if you use a steam mop. A page that doesn't name this loses them.
- Vinegar as default hardwood recommendation — acid degrades polyurethane over time.
- Ammonia or bleach on grout — degrades colored grout, leaves dirt-attracting film (CR documents this).
- Abrasive cleaners on stainless — dulls the finish.
- "One cleaner for everything" framing — mental red flag.

#### Recurring concerns
- "Did I already ruin my [surface]?" — anxious search pattern. They'll click a page that tells them how to assess existing damage first.
- "Does this void the flooring warranty?" — quiet but decisive factor.
- Long-term residue buildup — wax haze on hardwood, soap scum on grout, hard-water stains on stainless.
- Third-party certifiers (EWG Verified, Made Safe, Leaping Bunny) — they trust these more than brand self-claims.
- Re-seal / re-coat cycles — grout needs resealing, some finishes need recoat, cleaner choice affects both.

#### Voice and page-structure fit
Tone: careful, expert-adjacent, warning-forward. Lead with the caution ("before you buy a steam mop, check if your flooring manufacturer voids the warranty"). Acknowledge the horror story their neighbor had. Use named authority sources heavily: Bona as manufacturer-endorsed, EWG as third-party certifier, CRI for carpet-adjacent chemistry, the actual flooring manufacturer's care guide. Do NOT lead with product recommendation; lead with decision framework ("is your floor engineered or solid hardwood? is your laminate pre-2018?").

Page structure: caution block → decision tree / what-to-check-first → safe picks tier → risky picks with exact caveats. Explicit "if your floor is X, use Y and NOT Z" mapping. Cite manufacturer warranty language where possible. This avatar rewards length + depth — a 2,500-word careful guide with 8 cited sources beats a 900-word punchy listicle. Include an "FAQ: Can I use this on..." section.

### Avatar: Event-Driven Restorer

#### Core job
"Something happened — we moved in / flood / wine on the rug / toddler vomit / pre-listing prep. I need to deep-clean hard, right now. Rent-vs-buy math says buy this time."

#### Emotional state at arrival
Urgent. Problem-triggered. Stressed by the state of the carpet / couch / floor. Weighing cost against scale of the problem. Not chronically researching — they're in a one-shot research sprint. Willing to spend real money if the solution actually works because it's solving an event, not an ongoing nice-to-have.

#### Typical traits
30-55. Homeowner or long-term renter with carpet-heavy flooring. NOT pet-primary (pet-stain carpet cleaning stays with BPP). Trigger is almost always: home purchase with inherited old carpet, pre-listing prep, annual spring clean, or one specific disaster (wine spill, toddler vomit, tracked-in mud after a storm). Has often rented a Rug Doctor before and is tired of the $40/day + $18.95/solution math.

#### What they search
`best carpet cleaner machine`, `bissell big green vs rug doctor`, `carpet cleaner worth buying vs renting`, `how to deep clean old carpet in new house`, `carpet cleaner for stairs`, `fast drying carpet cleaner`, `IICRC certified carpet cleaning`.

Mental model: "I've rented a Rug Doctor 3 times in 2 years. At what point is buying one cheaper, and which one actually works?"

#### Budget and decision factors
Budget band: **$150-250 entry (mid-size uprights like Bissell ProHeat), $300-450 mainstream (Bissell Big Green class), up to $600 prosumer.** Rug Doctor rental is ~$40/day + $18.95 solution. Break-even lands around rental #4-6.

Cares about: tank capacity (Big Green 1.75gal vs Rug Doctor 3.9gal — matters for whole-house vs single-room), dry time (Big Green "dries in 2 hours" vs Rug Doctor "wet for hours or over a day" — THE most-cited differentiator), ease of filling/emptying, heated-water, weight and haulability (stairs), hose length (upholstery reach), storage footprint. Exclusive trust lane: IICRC S100 + VacuumLand forum head-to-head comparisons + Reddit "moved into a house" threads.

Does NOT care about: max water PSI, brand prestige, noise (they run it when house is empty).

#### Dealbreakers
- Carpet still wet 24 hours later. Core differentiator, most-searched complaint.
- Tanks that require mixing in a bucket first (Rug Doctor's specific drawback).
- Machine too heavy to get up stairs.
- Solution that voids the carpet manufacturer warranty.
- Pet-stain-only marketing — they are actively NOT a pet-mess buyer and find the framing irrelevant or off-putting.

#### Recurring concerns
- DIY vs pro honestly framed. IICRC S100 recommends annual pro cleaning of wall-to-wall carpet. They're weighing: buy + semi-annual refresh + rent pro annually, or just pro for everything.
- "Does it actually get out old set-in stains, or just surface dirt?" — inherited-carpet case.
- Storage commitment — a full-size Big Green lives in the garage at 40lb.
- Recurring solution cost — proprietary solutions can be $15-20 every few months.
- "Is my carpet even worth cleaning, or should I replace?" — existential decision. A page that acknowledges this is more trusted than one that assumes the answer is "clean it."

#### Voice and page-structure fit
Tone: practical, ROI-aware, honest about the Rug-Doctor-vs-buy math. Lead with the buying-trigger framework ("you should buy one if you'll use it 3+ times a year; rent otherwise"). Acknowledge the hybrid path — some households benefit from owning entry-level + hiring pro annually. Use VacuumLand comparison data and IICRC S100 cadence as exclusive authority anchors (neither shows up in other TCR desks). Name the dry-time differentiator explicitly. Avoid pet-centric examples.

Page structure: "rent vs buy" decision block → top pick + reasoning → Bissell-vs-Rug-Doctor head-to-head as named section → stairs-and-upholstery subsection → pro-vs-DIY-vs-hybrid framing → maintenance-and-storage realities. Machine-comparison table: tank capacity, dry time, weight, hose length, proprietary-solution cost, warranty.
